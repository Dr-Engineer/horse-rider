## The most important honest finding

**Horseback melee racing is a game invention.** No source describes racers fighting each other.
Real mounted competition was speed, target shooting at a gallop and stunts. Real duels were on foot.
Real raiders preferred ambush and theft. The game therefore frames itself as a **tall tale told by an
old rider**, which licenses the cartoon violence and later the impossible visitors while keeping the
voice Tibetan.

## Racing and festivals

- **Gyantse:** contests in horse racing, archery and shooting from horseback are said to date to the
  1400s (the 1408 date comes from a commercial source). Inter-village rivalry, a tent city on the plain,
  picnics, trade, wrestling, opera.
- **The Gallop Behind the Fort (Dzong-gyap), Lhasa, 26th of the first month:** Yaso cavalrymen fired a
  matchlock at one target beside a narrow runway, swung the gun round, then used a bow on the next.
  Prizes were **scarves graded by score**. Food booths lined the course. Best real precedent for a
  weapon-swapping gallop.
- **Lhasa New Year:** horse races without riders, foot races, wrestling (1923 newspaper report and
  Bell-era material). No route could be confirmed.
- **Nagchu, Damxung, Litang:** August grassland festivals with yak races, stone lifting, tug of war,
  opera and dance. Riders hang off the saddle to scoop silk scarves at speed. Handstands and hiding
  under the belly are in modern reports.
- **Track features from real sources:** a narrow mud-and-rock jump ramp, jumping a scarf through a
  hoop, a red-cloth pole target, scarf scooping.
- **Horses:** the Nangchen is reported as the commonest Tibetan racehorse. The Riwoche is a dun pony.
  Horses are decorated with ribbons, flags and plumes. Races show who owns the best horse.

## Gesar of Ling, the founding race

Joru, a scruffy exiled trickster with his mother Gogmo in Lower Ma, comes to his uncle Trothung's roof
as a raven and "prophesies" a horse race. Trothung believes it will make him king and bets everything:
property, gems and Sengcham Drugmo. His horse is Turquoise Bird. Joru catches the wild Kyang Goe and
wins. Our story echoes this: a scruffy nobody, a vain rich rider, a race bigger than it looks.

## Lungta and sacred etiquette

- **Lungta (rlung rta, wind horse)** is personal fortune and vitality. Tibetan speech treats it as
  something that flourishes or falls. That honestly supports a fortune meter, as long as it isn't
  presented as magic.
- Flags run **blue, white, red, green, yellow** in the most common order. They hang across passes and
  high places. Sets of five.
- Mani walls and chortens are passed **with the object on your right**. **Bon practice reverses this.**
  Mani stones should not be moved.
- Script and sacred images are not put on the floor or stepped on.
- Body hierarchy: head high, feet low.

## Arms

A rider's traditional set, the *'khor gsum*, was arrow, sword and spear.

- **Sword (ral gri):** straight, single-edged, 70-90 cm, trefoil pommel with a coral or turquoise bead,
  wire-wrapped grip, small cupped guard. Worn across the belly through the belt.
- **Spear:** iron head, long shaft bound in spiral iron, 3-3.5 m, yak-hair tuft and silk streamers.
- **Roped spear (thag mdung):** about 1.5 m, thrown from horseback and hauled back by rope. Documented,
  and our signature ranged weapon.
- **Sling ('ur rdo):** yak hair, diamond cradle, side-swung, a herding tool and a weapon.
- **Bow:** wood or composite, case at left hip, quiver at right hip, arrows stick well out of the quiver.
- **Shield:** round cane or imported leather.
- **Dob-dob kit (Goldstein 1964):** iron key on a long thong that outreaches a sword, thrown crescent
  knife, gravel-filled pen case. No evidence for meteor hammers or stone keys.
- **Weighted whip** and lead-weighted rope have modest support.
- **Saddle:** wooden tree, tall pommel and cantle, pierced metal plates on fine ones, paired wool rugs
  about 62 x 120 cm with a central medallion, iron or silver stirrups. A **long red breast-band tassel**
  marks rank.
- **Real rock-paper-scissors:** key outreaches sword, sword wins once inside, spear outreaches both but
  is awkward close up, sling and bow punish at range.

## Dress and identity

Evidence-based reads, used for the cast in `WORLD_AND_CAST.md`:

- **Khampa:** big robe hitched high, red belt, long sword through sash, tall boots, hair wound round the
  head with red cord, heavy earring.
- **Golok men** often kept short tousled hair, no hat.
- **Amdo:** fleece-lined gown with coloured borders, peaked hat with white lambskin brim and red tassels;
  women wear 50-100 plaits on a heavy back panel of cloth, shells and silver ("108 braids" is a modern
  label).
- **Changpa:** lokbar fleece robe with long hanging sleeves, fox hat, women's dark doja face paint.
- **Lhasa:** rank is wearable. Silk versus broadcloth, saffron versus indigo, disc hat with fur brim,
  long left-ear sochi earring.
- **Dob-dob:** horn-shaped ear locks, soot and grease around the eyes, red arm cloth. Also hired as
  escorts in travelling clothes, which is how we include one without an ordained monk's robes in combat.
- **Women rode astride as a matter of course** (Rijnhart: "I rode astride, as all Tibetan women do") and
  led fighters in Kham in the 1930s to 50s. No record found of women in formal festival races, which is
  fine in a tall tale.
- **Boots:** knee-high, thick stacked soles, banded uppers.

## History and tone

- **1954 is a Wood Horse year.** Horse years have a living link to Kailash pilgrimage.
- **1951 to about 1955 was an odd interval in central Tibet:** the PLA present, the Lhasa government still
  running things, soldiers paying in silver ("It is raining silver dhayen!"), trade booming. Violence in
  Kham and Amdo began about 1955-56 and reached Lhasa in March 1959.
- **The road:** motor roads reached Lhasa in 1954. The research recommends showing survey stakes, a far
  dust plume, then one truck, with no comment.
- **Comedy targets vanity, rivals and visitors, never the loss.** *Tintin in Tibet* is loved partly
  because it has no villain. *Far Cry 4* and *Uncharted 2* are criticised for exoticism. Tibetan satire
  (*zurza*, Uncle Tonpa, the clever underdog humiliating the rich) is a home-grown comic model that suits
  Road Rash trash talk.

## Road Rash and mounted-combat lessons

- **Road Rash II structure:** five courses in any order, 14 rivals, finish top three on all five to raise
  the level. Courses come back longer, prizes and fines bigger, rivals better mounted.
- **Combat:** one attack button plus direction gives punch, backhand or kick. Hit a rider as they swing
  out to take their weapon. Riders on low health fall back and return.
- **Crashes:** you are thrown off, then run back to the bike. Police can catch you after a crash, which
  costs a fine. You lose the game if you can't pay.
- **Bike classes:** light (fast, nimble, easily kicked over), nitro (burst), super (heavy, stable).
- **Rivals:** pre-race provocation or tips create a story. Biff is hated, Natasha is liked. Riders ride
  differently as well as talk differently. Handling scales with current speed.
- **Mount & Blade:** speed adds damage; a lance levelled at a gallop hits much harder.
- **Shadow of the Colossus:** the horse feels alive, but the team dropped disobedience because it was
  annoying. Make the horse feel alive through animation, not by ignoring input.
