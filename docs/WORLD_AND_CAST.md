# World and cast

## Frame story

An old rider, years later, tells a grandchild about the Wood Horse Year. Every race is told by them,
which is why it's so violent and why they always win. The voice is warm, boastful and funny.
When the child asks what happened afterwards, the old rider pours more tea. That silence does the work.

- Playing **Joru**, the narrator is **Pola** (grandfather). Playing **Yangkyi**, it is **Mola** (grandmother).

**Rule:** the tale never mentions 1959. The world quietly changes across the seasons: survey stakes in
Season 2, far dust plumes in Season 3, one parked truck beside the road at the final race. No one comments.
A short, factual card after the credits points to Tibetan sources.

## The story

**The Wood Horse Games.** It's a Horse Year. Pilgrims are on the roads, wool prices are high, and traders
have silver to burn. The rich wool dealers and noble houses have agreed on a great wager: a circuit of
race meets from the Kham gorges to the Western Gate of Lhasa. There are no rules worth mentioning.

**The rival.** Pon Wangyal Dorje, a chief's son, has never lost and tells everyone so. Like Trothung in the
Gesar story, he's backing the games because he expects to win. His horse is Turquoise Bird, named after
Trothung's horse by someone who hadn't read to the end of the story.

**You.** A scruffy nobody on a borrowed pack pony named Old Tsampa, in debt to an aunt, the Kalimpong wool
dealer Ama Choekyi. The villagers call you Joru after the snot-nosed hero of the epic, and don't mean it
kindly. Yangkyi gets the same treatment, for the same reason.

### Season beats

- **Spring Meets:** "I was seventeen and owned nothing but debt." Nobody notices you. Karma Tobgye, the old
  caravan guard, gives you advice and then hits you. Wangyal sends over a tea brick out of pity.
- **Summer Festival:** People have started to bet on you. Ama Choekyi raises the interest. Lithang Samdup
  wants to know your secret, and Serkhar Tashi Gyaltsen offers to buy your horse with money he doesn't have.
  Survey stakes appear beside the road. Nobody knows who put them there.
- **Harvest Final:** Wangyal stops laughing. At the Western Gate it's you against him. Win, and Wangyal
  arrives at your tent next morning with a barrel of chang: "You cheated. Let's race again." The old
  rider smiles. "We raced for the rest of that year."
- **After the credits:** The child asks, "And then?" The narrator pours tea.

- **Joru** (nickname, real name Tashi), Kham. Balanced. Hair wound with red cord, plain maroon robe.

One rider in the cast keeps Bon practice: **Sonam Phurba** passes sacred sites on the other side and
gets the same fortune for it. No one remarks on it.

### Rival voice samples

- **Wangyal, first meeting:** "A pack pony? Good. I need something to overtake."
- **Wangyal, grudge:** "You knocked my hat off in front of my mother. We're friends now. I'm going to kill you."
- **Karma:** "Watch the third bend. No, I won't tell you why."
- **Samdup:** "If I fall off, it was on purpose."
- **Temba:** "Under by-law nine, you may not overtake a sixth-rank official. By-law nine is new."
- **Pema:** "You rode well! Sorry about the arrow."
- **Dukpa:** "After you. No, truly. Mind the ditch."
- **Thupden:** "My horse owes nobody. Neither do I. You owe me a hat."
- **Drukmo Kyi:** "They named me for a prize. I've come to collect it."
- **Genden:** "Your colour is bad. Have you been eating enough butter? Also, I would duck."
- **Diki:** "In Calcutta they have machines that do this without the smell."
- **Chakri:** "That hurt! Good. Whoever you are, we're friends now."
- **Serkhar:** "I will pay you back for that. I will pay everyone back. Eventually."
- **Ama Choekyi:** "You owe me eighty srang. Ride accordingly."

### Crash and race lines

Shown as big comic words, kept short: THWACK, CLANG, OOF, UNSEATED, DOWN!, TEETERING, KI KI SO SO!, and
"HANDS DOWN" on Quiet Ground.

## End card (after credits, plain text)

> This story is a tall tale. Tibetans raced horses and shot at targets at a gallop. They did not, as far as
> anyone knows, hit each other with iron keys.
>
> In 1954 motor roads reached Lhasa. In the years that followed, this way of life was changed by war and
> exile. Many Tibetans still ride and race, in Tibet and beyond.
>
> To hear Tibetans tell their own history, start with the Tibet Oral History Project and Tsering Shakya's
> "The Dragon in the Land of Snows".
